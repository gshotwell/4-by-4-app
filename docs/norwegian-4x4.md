# Why the Norwegian 4x4?

Four intervals of four minutes, hard enough that you can't talk, with three minutes of easy movement in between. Warm-up and cool-down included, it's under 45 minutes. It came out of the Cardiac Exercise Research Group (CERG) at NTNU in Trondheim, and it's probably the most-studied single interval workout there is.

The case for it rests on three claims. The first is very well supported, the second is well supported in healthy adults, and the third is plausible but unproven.

## 1. VO2max is one of the best health markers we have

VO2max is the most oxygen your body can use per minute. It's set mostly by how much blood your heart can pump per beat (stroke volume), and it tracks long-term health remarkably well. The standard reference is Kodama et al.'s 2009 meta-analysis in *JAMA*: 33 studies, about 103,000 people. Each 1-MET increase in measured fitness (roughly 1 km/h faster running pace) was associated with about **13% lower all-cause mortality** and **15% fewer cardiovascular events**. People in the lowest fitness group had about 70% higher mortality than those in the highest.

That's observational, so it doesn't prove that *raising* your VO2max buys you the same benefit. But fitness predicts outcomes about as well as blood pressure, cholesterol or smoking, and unlike your genes it responds to training.

## 2. Hard four-minute intervals raise VO2max efficiently

The foundational trial is Helgerud et al. (2007, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*). Forty moderately trained men were randomised to one of four programmes, all matched for total work and done three times a week for eight weeks:

- long slow distance at about 70% of max heart rate
- lactate-threshold running at about 85%
- 47 × 15-second intervals at 90–95%
- **4 × 4 minutes at 90–95%** with 3-minute active recovery

Only the two interval groups improved VO2max significantly; the 4x4 group gained about 7%. Stroke volume rose about 10%, again only with intervals. The same total work done more gently didn't produce the same adaptation. Intensity mattered, not just volume.

### Why four minutes?

When you start a hard effort, heart rate and oxygen uptake take a minute or two to rise. Short intervals end before you reach the top. Four minutes gives you roughly two minutes at 90%+ of max heart rate per interval, which is where the heart is pushed to fill and empty fully on every beat. That's the stimulus that grows stroke volume. Three minutes of easy recovery brings heart rate down enough to repeat the effort three more times, so you get about eight to ten minutes near your ceiling in one session. Hardly anyone can hold that continuously.

## 3. It might help you live longer, but that's not proven

The tempting story is "VO2max predicts lifespan, the 4x4 raises VO2max, so the 4x4 extends lifespan." The best direct test is **Generation 100** (Stensvold et al., *BMJ* 2020). About 1,500 Norwegians aged 70–77 were randomised to five years of twice-weekly 4x4-style intervals, moderate continuous exercise, or national activity guidelines. Five-year mortality was:

| Group | Died within 5 years |
|---|---|
| 4x4 intervals | 3.0% |
| Control (guidelines) | 4.7% |
| Moderate continuous | 5.9% |

The direction is encouraging, but **the differences were not statistically significant**, and the trial's main finding was that supervised exercise (both kinds combined) didn't change mortality compared with following the guidelines. The control group muddied things: about one in five did regular high-intensity exercise on their own, and everyone in the study was healthier than their age group. An honest reading is that it's consistent with a benefit from high-intensity training in older adults, without demonstrating one.

Clinical results show the same pattern. A small 2007 study in heart-failure patients (Wisløff et al., *Circulation*) found 4x4-style training raised peak VO2 by 46%, against 14% for moderate exercise. The larger multicentre follow-up, **SMARTEX-HF** (Ellingsen et al., *Circulation* 2017, 261 patients), found HIIT **was not superior** to moderate training. Part of the reason was that about half the HIIT patients trained below their target intensity.

That last detail is the practical lesson.

## The catch: you have to actually hit the zone

The protocol's benefit depends on spending real time at 85–95% of max heart rate. That's hard to judge by feel:

- **Interval 1 tends to undershoot.** Heart rate lags behind effort, so a lot of the first interval is spent climbing. A 2020 study of the protocol (Acala et al.) found people spent about 2.7 of 4 minutes in zone in interval 1, against about 3.4 minutes in each later interval.
- **Later intervals tend to drift.** Fatigue and heat push heart rate up at the same pace, so you need to ease off to stay under 95%. In the same study, work rate fell from about 75% of peak power in interval 1 to 54–63% in intervals 2–4.
- **Your max heart rate is probably wrong.** Age formulas like 220 − age or 208 − 0.7 × age typically miss by about 10 bpm, which is enough to move every zone into the wrong place.

This app exists to deal with those three problems. It reads a chest strap (much more accurate than a wrist sensor at high intensity), tells you the moment you leave the zone, gives you a grace period while heart rate catches up at the start of each interval, and calibrates your max from your own sessions, or from an optional all-out final interval.

## How to use it

- **Twice a week** is what most of the studies used for health; three times in the shorter VO2max trials. More isn't obviously better: keep the other days easy.
- **The first interval should feel too easy for about a minute,** then hard. Don't sprint to make up for heart-rate lag; build into it.
- **"Hard" means you can say a few words, not sentences.** If you can hold a conversation, you're below the zone. If you're gasping by minute two, you went out too hard.
- **Recover actively.** Walk or jog lightly rather than stopping; it clears lactate and keeps the next interval's ramp shorter.
- **Track the 1-minute heart-rate drop** after each interval in the summary. Over weeks, a bigger drop is one of the clearest signs your fitness is improving.

If you have a heart condition, are new to hard exercise, or are coming back from illness, talk to a doctor before doing near-maximal intervals. The clinical trials that used this protocol in patients were supervised.
